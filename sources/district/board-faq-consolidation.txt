FAQ on Consolidation


The HUUSD School Board has begun (very) preliminary discussions of consolidation of schools in the HUUSD school district. This is a complex issue, and our community naturally has many questions about consolidation.  This FAQ is a first step towards answering questions and will be updated as we work through this process.  We welcome input from our community at every step of the way.


This FAQ addresses why we are considering consolidation (Part A), what changes to our schools the School Board is considering (Part B), the costs and savings of consolidation (Part C), the consolidation process (Part D), and how to get more involved (Part E).  


Part A: Why are we considering consolidation?


Q: Is consolidation a done deal? Is it happening without community input?


A: No and no! 


We are just at the beginning of this long process, which includes public presentations as well as School Board meetings and committee meetings.  All School Board meetings are open to the public, with time for public comment. Everyone is welcome to attend School Board meetings in person or on Zoom, and all meetings are recorded and posted on YouTube.  Committee meetings are on Zoom, again with time for public comment, and are posted on YouTube.


Vermont has strict open meeting laws, so all discussions and board votes on consolidation will happen at board and subcommittee meetings. You can see a thorough discussion of the Building Use and Visioning Committee's preliminary discussion on consolidation here.


Q: Why is the School Board considering consolidation? Are there other options the School Board should be considering?


A: The short answer is we're considering consolidation because our budget outlook is unsustainable.  After three consecutive years of cuts with a fourth year looming, we've run out of places to cut that don't severely hurt our students.  We need a new approach to avoid "death by a thousand cuts."


In more detail, the School Board has cut the HUUSD budget for the past three years, resulting in cuts of 15% from a level service budget (a budget that provides the same student services as the previous year).  We've had to make these cuts because of huge increases in health care costs (35% in 3 years!), special ed and mental health costs, and transportation/utility costs.  We're mandated to cover these costs, even as we face the declining enrollments common across all of Vermont and the US. If we had passed all these increases along to the school budget without making cuts, you would have seen your property taxes increase much more than they already have. As a result, we've cut more and more student services, including loss of the elementary world language program, cuts to nursing staff, and consolidation of PreK classes.  We've also cut administrative and other staff positions.  At the same time, we’ve been forced to postpone all but the most urgent repairs to our schools, leaving us with only about a million dollars for emergencies versus the $100,000,000 worth of identified maintenance needs. 


The combination of declining enrollments and increased costs puts us between a rock and a hard place. One Warren kindergarten school this year will have eight students, making it both expensive and not optimal for the students’ socialization.  Also, every cut we make increases our teachers’ workload.  For example, cutting back on nursing staff means teachers end up literally putting bandaids on kids during time they had previously spent on prep time.  So while we’ve tried to avoid cuts that directly impact what happens in the classroom, these cuts have already indirectly hurt our kids’ education.


We understand that the increases we've all seen in property taxes are unsustainable.  At the same time, we've run out of places to cut that don't directly affect student programs, so this year's cuts may involve athletics, theater, non-state mandated courses (like Advanced Placement classes), etc.   


All in all, we need a new approach to funding public education in our district.  Consolidation is an option we have to explore at this point.  We frankly don't know of another option.


Part B: Which schools will be affected by consolidation?


Q:  What schools will be closed if we consolidate?


A: Again, no decisions have been made.  Based on the extensive work done by our consultant TruexCullins, the Building Use and Visioning subcommittee is considering the following changes:
* Move Crossett Brook Middle School students to the Harwood Middle School
* Move Brookside students to Crossett Brook, and close Brookside
* Combine the four Mad River Valley elementary schools into one new school


Q: That's a lot of change!  How is this justified?


A: Moving middle school students from Crossett Brook to Harwood Middle School means that all middle school students will be in one location. Having one larger middle school both saves money because of staffing and maintenance efficiencies, and creates more opportunities for students. 


Brookside is over 100 years old and was constructed as a high school, not an elementary school. It will need extensive renovations, raising the important question of whether this is a good use of school district resources.  


Having four elementary schools (Fayston, Moretown, Waitsfield, Warren) in fairly close proximity in the MRV is not very efficient, although each of the schools has important roles in their town. Having one elementary school for the approximate 450 students in these schools will save money in the long run, as well as offering these students more academic and extracurricular opportunities.


Q: What are the plusses and minuses for closing schools?  Are there unknowns and opportunities?


A: The big plus is cost savings in the long run, which will be discussed below.  In general, having fewer, larger schools is more efficient, and takes the pressure off the huge repair and maintenance costs we face at our older schools, like the recent roof repairs at Brookside, the estimated costs of repairs at the Fayston Elementary School, and the PCB issue at the Warren Elementary School. On the academic side, as mentioned above, larger schools in general offer students more academic and extracurricular opportunities. 


There are definite downsides.  Some students will have increased transportation times, and towns will have to compensate for the loss of their schools' social function.  Consolidation would likely result in a reduction in the overall number of staff positions. We recognize the impact this could have on employees who have dedicated years of service to our students and communities. The number and types of positions affected cannot be determined at this stage.  


There are also effects that will depend on the student.  Some students will thrive with the chance to meet new friends, while other students do better in a smaller school.


A big unknown is the future use of closed schools.  Towns have the right to purchase a closed school building for $1.  This is an opportunity for towns to convert the school into community space and much needed apartments.  This again needs a lot of discussion. 


Another unknown is the effect of school closings on attracting young families to the district.  One argument is that the current small elementary schools are attractive, while the opposing argument is that one new, larger school is more attractive.
Q: Would a consolidated Valley elementary school be too large?
A: A consolidated Valley elementary school is currently estimated to enroll approximately 450 students, plus prekindergarten students. That would be larger than any of our current elementary schools including Brookside, which enrolls approximately 300 students. The Board recognizes that this would represent a significant change for students, families, and staff.
At the same time, an enrollment of approximately 450 students is not unusually large for an elementary school and falls within ranges examined in recent Vermont education policy analyses. Vermont’s 2023 Report on Education Financing identified 300–500 students as the most efficient range for an elementary school, while noting that fewer than 10 percent of Vermont elementary schools were within that range (Vermont Legislature, 2023). In 2025, the Vermont Superintendents Association similarly presented research identifying approximately 500 students as an effective elementary-school size and recommended that Vermont consider 300 students as a minimum policy goal (Vermont Superintendents Association, 2025). These findings do not suggest that enrollment alone determines educational quality or school climate. Rather, they indicate that a school serving approximately 450 students can operate at an educationally appropriate scale while still being intentionally organized to provide a personal, supportive experience for students.


Q: Where would the new Valley elementary school be?


A: This is to be determined.  While the current Waitsfield elementary school is the most centrally located, it can probably not be expanded further.  Problems include the structural inability to add a second floor, the limit on parking lot expansion, and the proximity to the Mad River in a time of climate change.  


Part C: What are the finances of consolidation?


Q:  How much money will consolidation save in the long run?  How much upfront expenses will there be?


A: According to this 2025 study by TruexCullins, consolidation would save us about $650,000,000 over 30 years.[1]  There are many assumptions that go into this figure, including average inflation rate, eventual replacement costs, etc., but in any case, it's a huge amount.  


The upfront costs are substantial.  A new elementary school to replace the Fayston, Moretown, Waitsfield and Warren schools would need a capacity of about 450 students and cost in the $55-75,000,000 range.  The cost of renovations needed to move Brookside elementary students to Crossett Brook and Crossett Brook middle school students to the Harwood middle school have not been priced out yet.


Q: How will the upfront costs be funded?


A: The big unknown is whether the State will offset some of the costs of construction and renovation, but the State certainly won't cover all the costs.  For the rest, the most likely route is to ask our communities to vote for a bond.  This will increase our property taxes in the short run (by a currently unknown amount), but will save us money in the long run.  


Please remember that our community members with limited resources can apply to property tax abatement.  And please also remember that without a bond, we could pay hundreds of millions of extra dollars in property taxes for school funding in our district.


Part D: How does the consolidation process work?


Q: What actions does the School Board have to take to put school consolidation in place?


A:  The School Board is governed by the Articles of Agreement adopted when HUUSD was created. Under Article 6(E), before closing a K–8 school, the Board must hold at least three public hearings on the proposed closure, including at least one in the community where the school is located. If the Board then intends to vote, it must provide at least ten days’ public notice of the vote and state the reason for the proposed closure. A school may be closed only by an affirmative vote of two-thirds of the Board of Directors. 


Q: So town residents don't get to vote on closing their school?  Didn't the residents of Worcester and Calais just vote on whether to close their schools? How is this fair?


A:  Different school districts have different Articles of Agreement.  Our Articles place the desires of the whole school district over the desires of the individual towns in the district. Worcester and Calais residents voted to keep schools open that are smaller than the smallest HUUSD school.  Our district simply cannot afford to keep our smallest schools going, and we don't know how these other schools will stay funded in the future.  


Q: Why isn't there a district-wide vote on consolidation? Isn't this anti-democratic?


A: The Articles of Agreement approved by voters when HUUSD was formed assign decisions about closing a K–8 school to the elected School Board rather than to a district-wide vote. As stated above, under Article 6(E), closing a school requires an affirmative vote of two-thirds of the Board. Before voting, the Board must hold at least three public hearings, including at least one in the affected community, and provide at least ten days’ public notice stating the reason for the proposed closure.
Just like our State Legislature, the Board is a representative body whose members are elected by voters in the district’s towns. Community members therefore participate through the election of Board representatives and through the required public-hearing process, but the Articles do not provide for a separate public vote on whether to close a school.
If consolidation requires voter-approved borrowing for construction or major renovations, voters would have a direct vote on that bond. A rejected bond could prevent that particular construction plan from proceeding, although it would not necessarily prohibit the Board from considering a different consolidation plan that does not require the same borrowing.  
Part E: I have strong opinions, and I want to be more involved and more informed with the consolidation process.  What can I do?


A:  To stay informed, attend or Zoom into school board meetings and the Building Use and Visioning committee meetings.  All School Board meeting times are posted here, and agendas are posted beforehand here.  The Building Use and Visioning committee meeting times and agendas are posted here.  All meetings have time for public comment.


There are many ways to get involved.  Attend board and subcommittee meetings, make public comments, post on Front Porch Forum or Facebook or Instagram, and keep in touch with your town School Board representatives.  


Strong opinions are welcomed! Your representatives don't operate in a vacuum. We need your input in order to come up with a best possible solution to our severe budget crisis.
