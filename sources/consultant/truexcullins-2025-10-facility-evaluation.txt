INTRODUCTION

The Harwood Unified Union School District serves six rural Vermont towns: Duxbury, Fayston, Moretown, Waitsfield, Warren, and Waterbury. The school district includes one PK-4th grade school (Brookside Primary School), three PK-6th grade schools (Fayston Elementary School, Waitsfield Elementary School, and Warren Elementary School), one K-6th grade school (Moretown Elementary School), one 5th-8th grade school (Crossett Brook Middle School), and one 7th-12th grade middle/high school (Harwood Union Middle & High School). Crossett Brook Middle School accepts 5th through 8th grade students from Duxbury and Waterbury. Harwood Union Middle School accepts 7th and 8th grade students from the Mad River Valley towns. Harwood Union Middle & High School was not included in the scope of this investigation.

PURPOSE OF THE STUDY

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the infrastructure of each building and site, make recommendations for its modernization, and estimate the costs for those upgrades. To do this, TruexCullins and a team of engineers inspected each building and reviewed previous drawings, reports and current permits. This document includes the building summaries and cost estimates.

In this instance, “modernization” means upgrading the building and site systems to current codes, technologies, and efficiencies. Often the recommendation is not for “replacement-in-kind”, but for a new, improved approach that utilizes modern technology. In addition to infrastructure modernization, this report also looks at concerns related to accessibility, building code, and state site regulations.

Please note that this report is a snapshot in time and the status and/or condition of any individual item may differ from the report findings.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

As you will see in the body of this report, there is a fair amount of work required to upgrade these schools. On average, the most recent major construction projects happened around 23 years ago, with a renovation at Warren Elementary School being the most recent (2017). This is significant because many building systems (HVAC, lighting, windows, flooring, etc.) have life spans in the range of 20-25 years or less. Continued use of systems beyond their design life often necessitates a crisis management approach to facility management, and at its worst, results in temporary school closings.

In general, these schools are in need of significant upgrades to meet current accessibility codes and energy standards. Many systems are near or past end of their useful life and will need replacement immediately or in the near future. To help visualize the condition of the schools across the district, a facilities condition heat map is provided on the following page.

Throughout the report, you will see reference to poor, fair, or good condition to describe current conditions of building systems and elements. These are defined as follows:

Poor Condition: Broken, non-functioning, hazardous, or obviously worn beyond expected life.

Fair Condition: Sufficiently functional, shows some signs of wear but still usable.

Good Condition: No obvious signs of wear, fully functional, or “like new”.

COST ESTIMATION

This part of the study is the estimation of the approximate costs for the required upgrades identified in the facility evaluations. Please note these figures do not include maintenance costs. These costs are organized into 3 categories:

Priority 1 – Items related to health, safety and welfare issues, which may include items like water intrusion, mold, code, accessibility or structural concerns. Items like this should get consideration in the short-term.

Priority 2 – Items related to energy or water efficiency gains and replacement of systems that are at the end (or beyond) their recommended usable life. This is often the bulk of the items in an aging building.

Priority 3 – Items that do not fit into either Priority 1 or 2 but would improve the overall

functioning of the facility.

BROOKSIDE PRIMARY SCHOOL

A BRIEF BUILDING HISTORY AND OVERVIEW

Brookside Primary School serves Pre-K through 4th grade students in Waterbury, Vermont. Currently, the school has approximately 303 students. The building is approximately 76,350sf. The original school building was constructed in 1898. In 1912 a second building was constructed to the North and in 1937 a third to the South. The last significant renovation to the building occurred in 2007, with renovations including but not limited to, the building connectors that combine the three larger building areas.

The parcel is approximately 10 acres. The site is bordered by residential properties along High Street to the west, Woody Avenue to the south, Hillcrest Terrace to the east, Stowe Street to the north. The main entrance to the school is accessed off from Stowe Street, with the main bus drop off area in front of the main entrance, at the north side of the building. Staff park behind the building in the south parking lots, while parents and visitors park in the lots to the north of the main entrance.

Brookside Primary School is a two-story masonry building with a basement level. The building consists of masonry bearing walls supporting wood floor and roof framing. The main building roofs are sloped, hip roofs with asphalt shingle roof covering and copper roof valleys. Roof areas over the gymnasium and entry/connectors are low-slope membrane roofs with internal roof drains and perimeter scuppers. Exterior walls consist of 12-14” of solid multi-whythe granite and brick masonry. Exterior openings consist of wood windows, which were restored during the last major renovation, hollow metal doors and frames, aluminum entry doors and frames, and a few original wood entry doors.

Interior partitions consist of plaster and lath over 2x wood framed walls. Flooring is a combination of vinyl composition tile (VCT) and carpet tile flooring, as well as, original hardwood flooring at many of the original building corridors, classrooms and offices. Ceilings are a combination of plaster or gypsum ceilings and drop-in acoustic ceilings (ACT). Interior doors are wood veneer and original stile and rail paneled doors.

The building is served by the municipal water and sewer systems. The building heating system is comprised of (3) oil-fired cast iron boilers serving the radiation and space heating components. Power is fed to the building via a 600-amp, 120/208V three-phase service. The school was renovated in 2007 and most mechanical and electrical systems were upgraded at that time. Interior lighting consists of linear fluorescent light fixtures that have been fitted with LED lamps. The building has an automatic fire alarm system and fire protection sprinkler system. The school is also provided with an emergency backup generator.

Brookside Primary School – Facility Evaluation – Site / Civil

Brookside Primary School is located at 47 Stowe Street, north of Waterbury Village. The school parcel is listed as 10 AC in size, and is surrounded to the north, west, and east by residential properties. The school building lies at the west end of the parcel, adjoining High Street. All of the school parcel lies outside of mapped flood zones.

Play areas are on the east side of the lot and parking to the south. A drop-off area and guest parking area extend along the north. Shade trees are concentrated at the north and west corners of the site. Soil mapping indicates silt loam below most of the property, with better drained Figure 1: Overall Site

soils along the eastern edge.

From the Stowe St / High St intersection the parcel rises 50’ to the east corner (Woody Ave. / Hillcrest Terrace Intersection). The front entry and lawn area slopes up to the front of the school at approximately 5%. Behind the school is a grassed bank with a 15% slope to the upper parking areas.

This location provides good access to the school from Main Street and convenient access to and from both Route 100 and Interstate 89. Primary access to the school is provided by a single curb cut on Stowe Street with secondary access to the rear of the school from Woody Avenue. High Street runs along the west side of the school building,

From Stowe Street, the front access loop takes parent vehicles counterclockwise and back out through the same curb cut. Bus traffic also shares this access and uses a dedicated bus drop off area east of the parent drop off loop. During morning drop-off school traffic caused queuing along Stowe Street with 10-12 vehicles noted in each direction.

The Waterbury Fire Chief noted several fire safety concerns at the school. During the afternoon pickup period, parents park along both sides of Stowe Street as well as High Street. This interferes with fire access for most apparatus. High Street becomes impassable for these vehicles. He also noted ongoing issues with parents parking in the fire lane in front of the school.

At the rear of the school parents dropped off students by looping through a section of the upper parking lot. A single car parked in the wrong parking spot could prevent this circulation from working. Student drop off in both locations was actively managed by school staff. IX | POTENTIAL STRUCTURAL IMPACTS OF FUTURE RENOVATIONS/ADDITIONS

A core concept to keep in mind when planning future use of space is to generally maintain shear walls. Eliminating such walls in a localized area would trigger the requirement for a global analysis of the lateral force resisting system and/or significant reinforcing of the localized area. This would likely require reinforcing of the lateral load resisting system and affect the cost of any proposed floor plan alterations. If wall removals or relocations are nonetheless desirable, costs for structural evaluation and design of lateral reinforcement should be included in any estimates.

Any changes to the roof, including the addition of new equipment or insulation would require further evaluation of the capacity of the existing roof framing, as well as possible reinforcing of the existing roof framing for the added loads.

A potential addition, taller than the existing building, could impact the existing building by creating a snow drift on the existing roof framing. This would require an evaluation, and possible reinforcing, of the existing roof framing for additional drifting snow loads from the new roof.

A BRIEF BUILDING HISTORY AND OVERVIEW

Crossett Brook Middle School serves 5th through 8th grade students in Duxbury, Vermont. Currently, the school has approximately 264 students. The building is approximately 69,930 gross SF between two levels and was originally built in 1996.

The parcel is approximately 30.75 acres. The site is bordered on the north by VT Route 100, on the west, south, and east by Crossett Brook, and on the northeast by residential property. The main entrance to the school is accessed off from VT Route 100, with the main entry drive directing traffic around to the southeast corner of the building and the main entrance to the school. The school has combined faculty and visitor parking along the east side of the building separating the school from the lower sports fields.

Crossett Brook Middle School is a two-story structure consisting of steel columns and beams and CMU bearing walls supporting steel floor and roof joists. The level 2 floor is a concrete slab on metal deck. Two main gable roof forms intersect over the main entry area and are covered with architectural asphalt shingles. Roof areas over the gymnasium, cafeteria, and exterior classrooms have a low-slope epdm membrane roofing system with internal roof drains. The main exterior façade is 4” brick and CMU masonry veneer over 6” metal stud framing with 1/2” gypsum exterior sheathing, R-19 fiberglass batt insulation, 6mil poly vapor barrier at the interior face of stud and 5/8” interior gypsum wallboard. Walls at larger spaces, such as the gymnasium, are 4” masonry veneer over 3” rigid polyisocyanurate insulation over 8” or 12” CMU block walls. Gable end walls are clad in pre-finished metal wall panels. High walls at double-height spaces use a translucent fiberglass panel glazing system. Exterior openings consist of aluminum-framed, dual-glazed windows, aluminum storefront windows at large main entry openings, and hollow metal exterior doors and door frames.

Interior partitions consist of gypsum wallboard over metal framed walls and CMU block walls. Flooring consists of vinyl composition tile (VCT) and carpet tile for a majority of the flooring. Ceilings are a combination of gypsum ceilings and drop-in acoustic ceilings (ACT). Interior doors are wood veneer or hollow metal in hollow metal frames.

The building is served by the municipal water and sewer systems. The building heating system is fed by (3) oil-fired, cast iron boilers. Power is fed to the building via a 1000-amp, three-phase, 277/480V service. Interior lighting consists of fluorescent light fixtures that have been fitted with LED lamps. The building has an automatic fire alarm system and fire protection sprinkler system. No emergency power generator was noted for the property.

IX | POTENTIAL STRUCTURAL IMPACTS OF FUTURE RENOVATIONS/ADDITIONS

A core concept to keep in mind when planning future use of space is to generally maintain shear walls. Eliminating such walls in a localized area would trigger the requirement for a global analysis of the lateral force resisting system and/or significant reinforcing of the localized area. This would likely require reinforcing of the lateral load resisting system and affect the cost of any proposed floor plan alterations. If wall removals or relocations are nonetheless desirable, costs for structural evaluation and design of lateral reinforcement should be included in any estimates.

Any changes to the roof, including the addition of new equipment or insulation would require further evaluation of the capacity of the existing roof framing, as well as possible reinforcing of the existing roof framing for the added loads.

A potential addition, taller than the existing building, could impact the existing building by creating a snow drift on the existing roof framing. This would require an evaluation, and possible reinforcing, of the existing roof framing for additional drifting snow loads from the new roof.

A BRIEF BUILDING HISTORY AND OVERVIEW

Fayston Elementary School serves Pre-K through 6th grade students in Fayston, Vermont. Currently, the school has approximately 98 students. The building is approximately 20,140 SF and was originally built in 1962. The school has undergone five additions over the years. In 1972 and 1980, additional classroom spaces were added. In 1984, two small support spaces were added. In 1992, a new main entry, main office, gymnasium/cafeteria, kitchen and art room were added. The last significant addition/renovation to the building occurred in 2005, with the addition of more classroom spaces.

The parcel is approximately 7 acres. The site is bordered on the south by German Flats Road, by forested residential properties to the west and north and residential homes on the east. The main entrance to the school is accessed from German Flats Road, with a short entry drive that directs traffic up to the main entrance along the west side of the school. The site slopes up from German Flats Road, with a plateau to the north where the sports fields are situated.

Fayston Elementary School is a one-story, wood-framed structure with reinforced concrete foundation walls and concrete slab-on-grade. The gymnasium and kitchen walls consist of CMU block and reinforced concrete bearing walls. The main roof structure is large glulam beams and timber decking at the original 1962 building and long-span wood trusses and structural insulated panels at the 2005 addition. All other eras of construction have structure covered by interior finishes and no construction documents available to reference. All roof surfaces have standing seam metal roofing. Exterior walls consist of natural cedar wood siding and trim. Exterior wall construction is unknown. Exterior openings consist of aluminum-framed windows, a few wood-framed windows, and hollow metal doors and frames.

Interior partitions consist of gypsum wallboard over 2x wood framed walls. Flooring consists of vinyl composition tile (VCT) flooring at classrooms, offices, gymnasium, and restrooms, and carpet tile flooring at some classrooms and corridors. Ceilings are a combination of gypsum ceilings, 2x4 drop-in acoustic ceilings (ACT), and exposed structural wood decking. Interior doors are wood veneer.

The building is served by a private water supply well and an on-site septic system. The building is heated by (2) LP gas-fired cast iron boilers feeding perimeter radiant heating units. Power is fed to the building via a 400-amp, single-phase, 120/240V service. Some of the buildings electrical distribution systems are nearing or at end of useful life. Interior lighting consists of linear fluorescent light fixtures that have been fitted with LED lamps. The building has an automatic fire alarm system and fire protection sprinkler system limited to storage rooms and the boiler room. No emergency backup generator was observed at this school.
